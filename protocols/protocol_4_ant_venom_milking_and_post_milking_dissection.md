# Ant Venom Milking and Post-Milking Dissection Protocol

> **Status: Draft for joint review, 19 October 2026.** Integrates the September hands-on protocol and Simon’s venom milking and gland dissection instructions. Unresolved differences are identified at the relevant steps and summarized at the end.


## Purpose

This protocol describes venom collection from live formicine ants using microcapillaries and a manual pump, followed by venom-gland dissection of previously milked ants. Pooled venom is intended for downstream molecular and proteomic analyses; dissected tissues are collected for transcriptomic work.

Following venom collection, milked ants are maintained separately for a working interval of **2 days** before dissection (Simon’s draft allows **2–3 days**; confirm). This allows venom and
transcriptomic samples originating from the same colony to be linked.

This protocol was established following practical training with Sarah.

---

## Experimental Overview

For each colony:

1. Select ants for venom collection.
2. Immobilize ants individually on ice.
3. Restrain each ant laterally in the milking apparatus.
4. Stimulate venom release.
5. Collect venom using a size-appropriate Drummond microcapillary (0.5 µL used in September training).
6. Transfer venom into a glass micro-insert.
7. Reset/siphon the pump.
8. Repeat with additional ants and pool the venom.
9. Place milked ants into a separate labeled recovery box.
10. Maintain milked ants for 2 days as the current practice (2–3 days in Simon’s draft).
11. Dissect the ants for transcriptomic sampling.

### Current Sampling Target

- Venom is pooled by colony/species.
- Current expected target: approximately **2 µL pooled venom per sample**.
- September estimate: **~30 ants** per pool; Simon suggests **at least 20 *Formica* or 40 *Lasius*** per colony to reach ~2 µL.
- The actual number of ants required depends on individual venom yield.
- *Formica rufa* is currently a priority species.

> **Important:** The final pooled venom target and required number of ants
> should be confirmed with Sarah/Simon before formal experimental sampling.

# Procedure

## 1. Preparation

1. Identify the colony/species to be sampled.
2. Assemble a compatible LABSOLUTE ND8 vial, VWR micro-insert and ND8 cap. Label the collection vial with researcher, date, colony ID and recorded venom volume.
3. Prepare a separate labeled recovery box/container for ants that have already
   been milked.
4. Record the colony ID and date of venom collection.
5. Clean the spring-holder apparatus with water and ethanol before use; clean again after venom spills and between colonies. Assemble the apparatus.
6. Prepare the manual pump and 0.5 µL microcapillary.
7. Check that the pump and capillary are functioning correctly.
8. Reset/siphon the pump before beginning the first collection.
9. Keep the glass collection tube immediately accessible and cold: on ice for short handling (Simon suggests no more than ~2 h), or on dry ice for longer handling.

> **Critical:** Venom droplets can dry rapidly. The collection system must be
> ready before venom release is induced.

---

## 2. Immobilization on Ice

1. Remove one ant from the colony.
2. Place the ant **directly on ice**.
3. Keep the ant on ice until it becomes sufficiently inactive for safe handling.
4. Once inactive, remove the ant from the ice.
5. Immediately transfer the ant to the metal milking apparatus.

The endpoint is sufficient immobilization for positioning and restraint rather
than a predetermined cooling time.

> Avoid leaving the ant on ice unnecessarily after adequate immobilization has
> been achieved. Simon notes that prolonged cooling can reduce venom expulsion in *Camponotus*.

---

## 3. Positioning the Ant

1. Place the immobilized ant onto the metal restraint apparatus.
2. Position the ant **on its side**.
3. Carefully position the movable arm of the apparatus against the ant.
4. Adjust the arm until the ant is sufficiently restrained to prevent major
   movement during stimulation.
5. Check the orientation of the ant before beginning venom collection. Simon’s draft advises **waiting for the restrained ant to wake fully before stimulation**; confirm this as the agreed endpoint.

> **CRITICAL STEP: The ant must be positioned laterally.**
>
> Incorrect positioning can allow released venom to contact the ant. Venom
> exposure may injure or kill the ant.

The ant should be restrained securely enough for manipulation but should not be
unnecessarily compressed or crushed.

---

## 4. Preparation of the Collection Capillary

1. Attach the chosen Drummond Microcap® (0.5 µL in September training) to the manual pump; record capacity for each sampling series.
2. Check that the capillary is securely attached.
3. Prepare the pump for aspiration.
4. Position the capillary close enough to the ant that released venom can be
   collected immediately.

Do not wait until venom has already been released to prepare the collection
system.

---

## 5. Induction of Venom Release

1. Position the microcapillary near the posterior end of the restrained ant.
2. Use the microcapillary/pump assembly to gently stimulate or irritate the
   posterior region.
3. Continue stimulation until venom release is observed.
4. If necessary, use fine forceps to gently apply pressure to the posterior
   abdominal region to encourage venom release.
5. Watch closely for formation of a venom droplet at the **acidopore (acidoporus)**. Simon recommends gentle forceps stimulation once the ant has awakened.

During initial training, an individual ant could release approximately one
visible droplet or slightly more. Actual yield can vary considerably between
individuals.

> **Critical:** Be prepared to collect the venom immediately after it appears.
> The small droplet can dry rapidly.

---

## 6. Venom Collection

1. As soon as a venom droplet appears, bring the microcapillary into contact
   with the droplet.
2. In September training, the manual pump was used to draw venom into the microcapillary. **Method to confirm:** Simon’s draft describes presenting the capillary to the acidopore for uptake; establish whether self-filling or pump-assisted suction is intended for each species.
3. Collect as much of the released venom as possible before it dries.
4. Remove the capillary from the ant once collection is complete.
5. Transfer the collected venom into the labeled glass micro-insert. Reuse a capillary only within the same colony if uptake remains reliable; **change capillaries between colonies** and sooner if contaminated or blocked (Simon).
6. Pool venom from ants belonging to the same intended experimental
   colony/sample.

Do not combine venom from different colonies unless explicitly required by the experimental design. Simon suggests repeating stimulation/collection **3–4 times per individual** until venom is exhausted and estimating collected volume using capillary graduations.

---

## 7. Pump Reset / Siphoning

After transferring the collected venom into the glass micro-insert:

1. **Siphon/reset the pump.**
2. Release/reset the pressure remaining from the previous collection.
3. Check that the collection system is ready for the next ant.
4. Only then proceed with the next venom collection.

> **CRITICAL STEP:** The pump must be siphoned/reset after each venom transfer.
> Residual pressure from the previous collection can interfere with uptake of
> venom from the next ant.

### Working Collection Cycle

**Cool → Position → Restrain → Stimulate → Collect → Transfer → Siphon/reset → Recover → Next ant**

Repeat this cycle for each ant contributing to the pooled sample.

---

## 8. Post-Milking Recovery

1. Carefully release the ant from the restraint apparatus.
2. Inspect the ant for obvious injury.
3. Transfer the milked ant to the designated post-milking recovery box, with **water and honey** provided (Simon).
4. Keep milked ants separate from ants that have not yet undergone venom
   collection.
5. Maintain colony identity throughout recovery.
6. Record any mortality, injury, or abnormal behavior.

Do not return milked ants to an unlabeled general colony container if they are
intended for subsequent transcriptomic sampling.

---

## 9. Continue Collection Until the Pool Is Complete

Repeat Sections 2–8 with additional ants from the same colony.

For every pooled venom sample, record:

- Species
- Colony ID
- Venom sample ID
- Number of ants milked
- Date of collection
- Final pooled venom volume
- Recovery box
- Mortality during/after milking
- Notes on unusual collection difficulty or yield

The September working estimate is **~30 ants per pooled sample**; Simon suggests a minimum of **20 *Formica*** or **40 *Lasius*** ants per colony for ~2 µL venom. These are not interchangeable fixed requirements.

Collection should ultimately be based on the required pooled venom volume and actual venom yield. Keep samples cold during processing and **store pooled venom at −80 °C** (S1 lab **E.01.0**).

## 11. Venom Gland Dissection

Dissect venom glands from previously milked ants for downstream molecular work, including transcriptomics. The following steps integrate Simon’s dissection draft and the confirmed working volumes.

## Dissection steps

1. After the designated recovery period, cool ants on ice (directly or in a Petri dish) until inactive. The **2-day versus 2–3-day interval** remains pending confirmation.
2. Label **two 2 mL tubes** with researcher, date, colony, tissue type (**venom gland tissue** or **carcass**) and number of individuals. Add **100 µL DNA/RNA Shield to each tube**. Keep samples cold during handling; Simon suggests ice for short periods (~2 h) and dry ice for longer periods.
3. Clean the microscope working area and slide with water and ethanol. Clean the slide between ants and thoroughly between colonies to reduce contamination.
4. Place an immobilized ant on the microscope slide and remove the head and thorax. Place these into the **carcass tube**.
5. Add **50 µL DNA/RNA Shield** onto the slide for this individual and orient the remaining abdominal section **on its back** to expose internal structures.
6. Carefully open or remove the cuticle at the posterior end of the abdomen.
7. Simon’s draft says to grasp a **“cord-like tissue”** in the terminal abdominal region and pull slowly while maintaining the anatomical arrangement at the gaster tip. **Pending Simon/Sarah clarification:** what exact structure is meant by “cord-like tissue”?
8. Identify the venom apparatus and associated structures using the anatomical features below before separating them from surrounding tissue.
9. Separate the venom gland apparatus as cleanly as possible from other abdominal structures. Simon notes that reservoir rupture may be acceptable at this stage, potentially even useful for RNA extraction; **the acceptable extent of rupture and handling should be confirmed**.
10. Transfer the dissected venom gland tissue and remaining abdominal tissue promptly into their **appropriate, separately labeled tubes**. **Pending:** whether any of the 50 µL slide solution is transferred with tissue.
11. Simon proposes pooling approximately **15 *Formica*** or **30 *Lasius*** venom gland samples and corresponding carcasses per colony for RNA extraction. Confirm this target against the final experimental design.

## Anatomical identification

| Structure | Appearance or relationship described by Simon |
|---|---|
| Venom reservoir | Translucent, balloon-like |
| Venom duct | Runs toward the gaster tip / acidopore |
| Convoluted gland | Milky structure on top of the reservoir |
| Glandular filaments | Two fine filaments emerging from the convoluted gland and floating in the haemolymph |
| Dufour’s gland | Adjacent, darker, unilobed or bilobed gland associated with the duct; avoid contamination |
| Hindgut | Nearby posterior abdominal structure; distinguish from venom tissues |
| Fat bodies | Glomerular-looking tissue around the venom apparatus |
| Tracheae | Tube-like structures |

**Outstanding anatomical observations from Simon’s draft:** Is the reservoir empty or partly full after milking? Are the filaments still attached to the convoluted gland, or have they been lost? Record these observations where possible rather than treating them as resolved.

## Dissection quality control and practical notes

- Pull gently; the reservoir and associated glandular structures tear easily.
- Minimize contamination with Dufour’s gland, fat bodies, hindgut and other abdominal tissues.
- Clean glass dissection surfaces thoroughly between samples and colonies.
- Minimize handling time and transfer tissue to preservation solution promptly for RNA work.
- A successful dissection should yield an intact or mostly intact translucent reservoir, associated duct/gland tissue, and minimal visible contamination.

# Sample Identification and Traceability

Venom collection and transcriptomic sampling should remain traceable to the
original colony.

A recommended naming relationship is:

`Colony ID → Venom sample → Post-milking ants → RNA sample`

Example:

`FRU01 → FRU01-V → 2-day recovery → FRU01-RNA`

If multiple biological replicates are collected:

`FRU01-V-R1`

`FRU01-RNA-R1`

The exact naming convention should match the laboratory's agreed sample
inventory system.

**Box ID + position + exact sample label**

# Troubleshooting / Practical Notes

### Ant moves during collection

The ant may not have been sufficiently immobilized or restrained.

- Return to ice briefly if necessary.
- Reposition the ant.
- Check the restraint before continuing.

### Venom contacts the ant

- Stop collection.
- Check the ant's orientation.
- Ensure subsequent ants are positioned laterally.
- Record any resulting injury or mortality.

### Venom dries before collection

- Prepare the capillary before stimulation.
- Keep the capillary close to the collection area.
- Collect immediately after droplet formation.

### Venom is difficult to release

- Continue gentle stimulation of the posterior region.
- If necessary, gently apply pressure with fine forceps.
- Avoid excessive force that could injure the ant.

### Capillary does not collect properly

- Check the pump pressure.
- Confirm that the pump was siphoned/reset after the previous collection.
- Check the capillary attachment.
- Replace the capillary if necessary, especially when changing colonies. Simon’s draft has an unfinished instruction about impaired capillary uptake; clarify before finalizing.

### Venom yield varies between ants

This is expected.

Record the number of ants required to reach the desired pooled volume rather
than assuming that every individual contributes the same amount.
